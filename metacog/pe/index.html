More Than Physical Performance

Why PE?

Physical Education is often misunderstood as a subject where students simply perform. In reality, students are constantly analysing, evaluating, adapting, communicating and solving problems.

Students often leave a lesson saying
“We played volleyball.”

The goal is not simply to learn sport. The goal is to learn through sport.

Students often remember the activity. Metacognition helps them recognise the learning.

What they also did
  • Made decisions under pressure
  • Tested strategies
  • Adapted their approaches
  • Provided evidence-based feedback
  • Considered different perspectives
  • Solved movement challenges
  • Reflected on success and failure

Lesson Architecture

Three Ways We Develop Thinking in PE

Thinking routines work best when they are woven organically into lessons, and when student reflections are collected, monitored, and met with feedback rather than left unguided.

“Metacognition is understanding how you learn best and using this knowledge to adapt your thinking to thrive in different situations.”
1. Awareness Recognising the task, goals, available strategies, and which Learner Ambitions and strengths are being used before, during, and after activity.
2. Regulation Monitoring effort, confidence, and performance in the moment and adjusting strategy when something isn’t working.
3. Transfer Applying content, skills, strategies, and attitudes both from PE into other school subjects and hobbies, and from those pursuits back into PE.
1

Through Performance

Students solve movement problems, make decisions and adapt strategies in real time.

2

Through Observation

Students observe peers, identify evidence and provide structured feedback.

3

Through Discussion

Students challenge ideas, explain reasoning and consider alternative viewpoints.

10 Thinking Routines
Claim, Support, Question
▼

Purpose: Move students from quick opinions to evidence-backed reasoning.

Instead of offering vague praise or unsupported statements, students make a clear claim about performance or technique, back it up with concrete support from what they observed, and pose a question to test whether that idea holds up or how the movement could be refined next.

In PE: Gymnastics Feedback (Partner or Video)

Well suited to giving feedback on a gymnastics routine, either watching a partner live or reviewing your own performance from a video, grounding observations in specific evidence rather than simply saying “good routine.”

See, Think, Wonder
▼

Purpose: Observe carefully before jumping to interpretation or advice.

This routine slows down observation by separating objective facts from interpretation. Students first state strictly what they see happening, explain what they think is driving it, and ask what they wonder about how things might unfold or be improved.

In PE: Unpacking a Tournament or Event

Can be used to describe and analyse a tournament or sporting event, noticing patterns in how games, teams, or competition structures play out before jumping to conclusions.

Creative Questions
▼

Purpose: Generate imaginative, open-ended questions to explore new possibilities.

Students brainstorm open-ended “What would it be like if...?” questions to challenge conventional rules and invent fresh variations of a game, encouraging divergent thinking before testing an idea in play.

In PE: Adapting Volleyball Rules

An example of explicitly setting Creativity as the target ambition, asking students to generate creative questions to adapt volleyball, such as:

“What would it be like if the ball was huge, the net was low, there were no boundaries, we could catch...?”

How Else and Why?
▼

Purpose: Develop flexibility, adaptation, and purposeful coaching communication.

Rather than treating drills or ways of explaining as fixed, students are challenged to think like coaches and adapt what they are doing, justifying why a different structure or approach works better.

In PE: Adapting Drills & Coaching Communication

Adapting a drill: Students are given a simple drill for a ball game (such as pass and move in football) and have to adapt it and explain why.

Coach communication: Asking “How else can I help my partner or team understand?” (for example, showing them vs. telling them).

Think That, Stand Back, Push Back
▼

Purpose: Examine initial assumptions and test them against evidence.

Students first capture an initial instinct or hypothesis (Think That), step back to ask what evidence actually supports it (Stand Back), and deliberately look for flaws, variables, or counter-arguments (Push Back).

In Sports Science

Well suited to Sports Science classroom lessons, such as examining experiment design, questioning assumptions, and spotting limitations in how data is collected.

What? So What? Now What?
▼

Purpose: Turn performance reflection into implications and concrete next steps.

Left unguided, students often stop at What? (describing what happened). Adding So What? pushes them to analyse the implications of their effort or strategy, while Now What? commits them to a specific adjustment for next time.

In PE

Well suited to reflecting on a cross-country run performance, examining pacing, effort, and energy management (often prompting students to identify Committed), as well as adapting tactics during unfamiliar games.

Think, Pair, Share
▼

Purpose: Give every student processing time before testing ideas with a partner and group.

Students first take time to form their own idea (Think), compare and refine it with a partner (Pair), and then share with the wider group before putting their solution into action (Share).

In PE: Blindness Simulation Glasses

Students try tunnel vision, blurry, and other simulators of blindness, individually think about how to adapt the rules to include somebody wearing the glasses, discuss with a pair, and then share their adaptation with the group before trying it out.

What They Said
▼

Purpose: Build active listening, empathy, and accurate perspective-taking.

Instead of sharing their own opinion, students must listen closely and accurately report their partner’s thinking to the group. Knowing they will represent someone else’s viewpoint encourages respectful dialogue and deeper attention during discussions.

In PE

Works well as a walk-and-talk warm-up discussing an issue, such as transgender inclusion in sport, or something lower-stakes like what we should change about our PE programme.

Headlines
▼

Purpose: Distil the core learning of a lesson into a single memorable statement.

Rather than answering “What did we do today?” with the name of a sport, students write a concise newspaper-style headline that captures the most important tactical, technical, or collaborative takeaway from the session.

In PE

Can be used in the plenary of any lesson as a quick, high-impact check on whether students recognised the learning behind the activity.

I Used to Think... Now I Think...
▼

Purpose: Make conceptual shifts explicit so students recognise how their thinking has evolved.

By comparing what they believed at the start of a block of work with how they understand the activity now, students become aware of their own learning progression rather than viewing the unit simply as a series of games or drills.

In PE

Used at the end of a unit to help students articulate how their understanding of tactics, skill execution, or teamwork has changed over time.

Language Habits & Teacher Feedback

Questions That Promote Thinking

Changing our questioning vocabulary across all teaching helps prompt better thinking, from “What happened?” to “What makes you say that?”

Essential Practice

Collect Reflections & Give Feedback

While our vocabulary can change throughout all teaching, we should not expect students to be able to reflect well automatically. When left alone, they tend to describe what happened rather than reflect. Collecting their reflections and giving timely feedback is what moves them from surface description to thinking about implications and connections.

Evidence

  • What makes you say that?
  • What evidence supports that?
  • How do you know?

Explanation

  • Why do you think that happened?
  • What led you to that conclusion?
  • Can you explain your thinking?

Adaptation

  • How else could you do it?
  • What would you change?
  • Why might that work better?

Transfer

  • Where else could this be useful?
  • What other activities use this idea?
  • How might this help outside PE?
Partner Observation & Feedback

Mosston Style C: Practice Style

Many of these routines fit naturally within partner observation and feedback tasks when one student practises a skill and a partner observes. The performer develops the skill. The observer develops the thinking.

Claim, Support, Question

Partners state a clear claim about a gymnastics skill or routine they observed (or reviewed on video), support it with specific evidence from the attempt, and ask a question to test what to refine next.

See, Think, Wonder

When watching a partner practise a skill, observers separate what they physically see happening from what they think is causing it, before wondering what adjustment might help next.

What Makes You Say That?

Asking a partner “What makes you say that?” moves peer feedback away from casual opinion and requires them to justify their observation with evidence.

How Else and Why?

Partners think about how else to communicate feedback or adapt a drill (for example, showing vs. telling) and explain why that change helps the performer.

Final Thought

PE develops thinkers as well as performers.

Remember: While our vocabulary can change throughout all teaching, we should not expect students to be able to reflect well on their own. We need to collect their reflections and give them feedback to move them from simply describing what happened to thinking about implications and connections.

Through deliberate routines, collected reflections, questioning, and structured feedback, students learn to justify ideas, challenge assumptions, adapt strategies, and transfer content, skills, strategies, and attitudes beyond the sports hall and playing field.

The goal is not simply to learn sport. The goal is to learn through sport.
